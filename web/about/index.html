How the Atlas works.

A facility-level 0–100 score for the climate risk children face at the school or clinic they depend on. This page explains what the score means, how it's built, and where it falls short.

Mission

Most climate-vulnerability assessments stop at the country or district level. But money gets spent on specific buildings, not countries. The Atlas pushes vulnerability scoring down to the facility level, so the question "which clinic should we harden first?" has a data-backed answer for any country.

The audience is the people who actually allocate adaptation budgets: country ministries of health and education, UNICEF country offices, NGOs running facility-strengthening programmes, and journalists covering the climate-equity beat.

The score

Each facility gets a single 0–100 number (the Child Climate Risk Score) that answers how dangerous is the climate becoming for the children who rely on this facility? The score is a weighted sum of six sub-scores, all 0–1, capturing the hazard, the population at stake, and the facility's ability to keep operating.

Sub-score weights

Sub-scoreWeightWhat it measures
Heat exposure25%Days/year above 35°C apparent temperature, from ERA5 reanalysis.
Air pollution25%PM2.5 30-day average + hours above the WHO 15 µg/m³ short-term guideline, from Copernicus CAMS.
Child-population density15%Under-18 catchment within facility access radius, from WorldPop + UN DESA shares.
Flood risk15%Heavy-precipitation days (≥50 mm) as a flood proxy. JRC global flood maps planned for v0.3.
Drought risk10%Longest consecutive dry-day run in the last year.
Facility fragility10%Power, water, structure priors from OSM tags. WHO SARA / UNICEF EMIS integration planned for v0.3.

Recommendations

Each facility gets up to 3 prioritised actions, generated by a rule-based system (not an LLM) for reproducibility and auditability. Each rule maps a measurable condition to a specific intervention, a priority, and a USD cost range. For example: "if 60+ consecutive dry days and the facility is a clinic, install a solar-powered borehole, $3,000–8,000." Rules live in pipeline/scoring/recommendations.py in the open-source repo.

Recommendations are ordered to mirror the facility's top drivers wherever a rule exists. Some drivers (like child-population density) appear in the panel only to explain the score. There's no facility-level fix for them, so they don't produce a recommendation.

Climate and air-quality values are sampled on a strided grid (one sample per N facilities) and nearest-neighbour interpolated for the rest. At Nigeria's scale (50K facilities) this is the only tractable way; the per-coordinate cache means re-runs cost only the new sample points.

Honest limitations

  • Prototype. v0.2. Nigeria is the most-polished country; Bangladesh and Guatemala follow. Methodology will tighten as we onboard more countries.
  • Facility fragility is heuristic. We infer power, water, and structure status from OpenStreetMap tags, which are uneven. WHO SARA + UNICEF EMIS integration on the v0.3 roadmap will replace this with real facility-audit data.
  • Flood is a proxy. We count heavy-precipitation days as a flash-flood proxy. JRC Global Flood Hazard maps are a more accurate signal and are planned.
  • Climate sampling is gridded. Facilities within ~1–5 km of a sampled point share that climate value. Climate varies slowly at this scale so the approximation is reasonable, but a denser stride (compute-heavy) would refine the upper tail of the score distribution.
  • Not for routing or compliance. The score is a prioritisation aid, not a regulatory verdict. A facility scoring 65 isn't safer than a facility scoring 70 by any single hazard. They're different blends.
